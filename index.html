What this is

Cylinder Designer is a calculator that designs and checks a hydraulic cylinder from end to end. You describe the job (how hard it must push and pull, how far, how fast, at what pressure) and the cylinder (bore, rod, tube, threads, pins). It then checks every part that can fail — in both directions, at the worst pressure the system can ever make — and gives each check a coloured result.

Use it two ways: to size a new cylinder (it tells you the smallest standard bore and rod that work), or to check an existing one (enter its real dimensions and see what passes). It works in mm·bar or in·psi — the switch at the top converts everything.

Before you start on a real cylinder — what to have in hand

  • The job: push force and pull force the load needs, the moving mass, whether it moves horizontally or up/down, stroke, wanted speeds.
  • The hydraulics: working pressure, the relief valve setting (the most important number — all strength checks use it), pump flow, and whether there is a flow control or counterbalance valve on the rod side.
  • Cylinder size: bore, rod diameter, stroke (nameplate or drawing).
  • Geometry: mounting style (clevis, trunnion, flange…), pin-to-pin length with the rod fully in, how far the rod sticks out of the head when retracted.
  • Parts: tube outside diameter, cap thickness, cap weld size, rod-end thread, piston thread, rod-end pin diameter and its length in the eye, cushion lengths with spear and collar diameters, port drilling diameters.
  • Materials: minimum yield strength of rod, tube, cap, pin and tie rods — from the material certs or Materials Atlas. The app never assumes a material; the numbers loaded are examples only.

Step 1 — Application: what the cylinder must do

Asks: push force, pull force, moving mass, direction, stroke, extend and retract speed, working pressure, relief pressure, efficiency, and whether the rod side can be metered out (blocked or throttled).

Plain meaning: push and pull are the loads the job needs, not counting the load's weight — pick "vertical" and the app adds or subtracts the weight for you. Working pressure is what does the job; relief pressure is the most the system can ever make.

Example: 80 kN push, 30 kN pull, 500 kg, horizontal, 800 mm stroke, 100 mm/s out, 150 mm/s in, 160 bar working, 200 bar relief.

Say it out loud: "We start from the job — the forces, the stroke, the speed and the relief setting — because everything else is checked against those."

Step 2 — Bore and rod

Asks: bore and rod diameter. The size lists offer standard ISO 3320 (metric) and NFPA (inch) sizes.

Tells you: the smallest bore that does the job, the smallest standard bore, the two piston areas, the area ratio φ, and a table of forces both ways.

Example: 100 mm bore with a 70 mm rod. The rod covers almost half the piston, so the rod side has 40.06 cm² against 78.54 cm² — φ = 1.961. At 160 bar that pushes 125.66 kN (80 needed) and pulls 64.09 kN (30 needed). The smallest workable bore shows 85.4 mm because the big rod eats into the pull area — a 90 mm bore would just do. At the 200 bar relief the cylinder can stall at 157.08 kN push and 80.11 kN pull: those are the forces every strength check uses.

Chips: Extension and Retraction force — OK when the force at working pressure covers what the job needs.

Say it out loud: "Force is pressure times area — the full piston pushes, only the ring around the rod pulls, and we check the parts at the relief pressure, not the working one."

Step 3 — Speed, flow and power

Asks: pump flow (optional) and pump efficiency.

Tells you: the flow each speed needs, the pump size, stroke times, oil per stroke, power, and the rod-side pressure if the oil there is trapped.

Example: 100 mm/s out needs 47.1 L/min; on that flow the rod comes back at 196 mm/s (faster, because the rod side is smaller). Coming back, 92.4 L/min pours out of the cap side — almost double the pump flow — so the cap port and return line must handle it. Power at 160 bar is 12.57 kW. If the rod side is metered out with no load, it can reach 200 × 1.961 = 392.2 bar — that is "intensification".

Chips: "Rod-side pressure when metering out" shows Check whenever it is above relief — the rod seals, head and tube must be rated for it. The tube check in step 5 already includes it.

Say it out loud: "Speed is flow divided by area, and the cap side dumps nearly twice the pump flow on the way back — and a trapped rod side can see almost double the relief pressure."

Step 4 — Rod buckling

Asks: mounting style, pin-to-pin length retracted, end fixity, rod stiffness and yield, required safety factor; optional rod stick-out and gland stiffness for the extra models.

Plain meaning: when the rod pushes, it is a long column and can bow sideways and fold — long before the steel is overstressed. The longer the cylinder and the thinner the rod, the worse.

Example: extended, the cylinder is 1,900 mm pin to pin. A 70 mm rod over that length can carry 663.78 kN before buckling (Euler — it is slender). The stall push is 157.08 kN, so the safety factor is 4.23 against the 3 you asked for — OK. 70 mm is also the smallest standard rod that passes.

Stop tube: a spacer that keeps the piston and rod bearing apart on long strokes. A rear clevis has a stroke factor of 2, so the "basic length" is 2 × 800 = 1,600 mm (63 in). Catalogues add 1 in of stop tube for every 10 in over 40 in → 76.2 mm (3 in), shown as Check because it is a recommendation.

Extra models (for information): the whole cylinder as one column — rigid joint 1,188.25 kN; DNV's formula 1,403.47 kN (factor 8.93, DNV wants 4). These are higher than the rod-only number on purpose — see "What it does not do".

Say it out loud: "The rod is a column; at full stall force it still has more than four times the load it would buckle at, and we judge it on the rod alone, which is the safe way."

Step 5 — Tube (barrel)

Asks: tube outside diameter, tube yield and stiffness, required safety factor, optional test pressure.

Tells you: wall thickness, stresses, the safety factor at relief, and — most important — the safety factor at the highest pressure any chamber can see.

Example: 133 mm OD on a 100 mm bore is a 16.5 mm wall. At 200 bar the factor is a comfortable 4.45. But the app also looks at intensification (392.2 bar) and the cushions; the rod-end cushion averages 415.5 bar, so the tube is judged there: factor 2.14 against 2 — OK. Under pressure the bore grows 34.1 µm, which opens the gap behind the piston seal.

Say it out loud: "We don't check the tube at relief only — we check it at the worst pressure it can ever see, which here is the rod cushion."

Step 6 — Cap and welds

Asks: cap thickness, how it is held (welded all round or simply supported), cap yield, weld throat size and allowable weld stress.

Example: a 30 mm cap bends to 42 MPa — factor 8.52; 14.5 mm would be the minimum. The weld carries the whole end load, 157.08 kN, at 47 MPa — factor 2.55 on its allowable.

Say it out loud: "The pressure tries to blow the cap off — the cap is checked in bending and the weld that holds it is checked in shear."

Step 7 — Rod, threads and pin

Asks: thread system, rod-end thread, optional neck diameter behind the thread, piston thread, pin diameter and length, pin yield.

Plain meaning: extending squeezes the rod; retracting stretches it. The thinnest parts in tension are the threads and any undercut.

Example: pulling 80.11 kN through an M42 × 2 rod thread is 63 MPa — factor 8.21. The piston thread (M36 × 2) holds the piston when the rod side is at relief: 80.11 kN, 88 MPa, factor 5.94. The rod body sees 41 MPa squeezing and 21 MPa stretching. The 40 mm pin in double shear sees 63 MPa — factor 5.91.

Say it out loud: "On the way back the rod is in tension, so we check the rod, its threads and the piston joint at the full stall pull."

Step 8 — Tie rods / bolts

Only for tie-rod or bolted cylinders. It splits the end load among the rods and checks each one's thread. The example is welded, so the cap weld in step 6 does this job and this step just says so.

Say it out loud: "On a tie-rod cylinder the tie rods hold the ends on, so each one carries its share of the full end load."

Step 9 — Cushioning

Asks: cushion length at each end, spear and collar diameters, and the pressure your cushion chamber is rated for.

Plain meaning: near the end of the stroke a spear or collar plugs the exit, the trapped oil brakes the piston. The pump keeps pushing while it brakes, so that energy counts too.

Example: at the rod end the moving load brings only 3 J, but the pump pushing at relief adds almost 3,926 J — total 3,929 J, giving an average 415.5 bar in the small ring around the collar. The cap end averages 122.0 bar. Both show Check until you type the chamber rating — the app will not guess it.

Say it out loud: "The cushion has to stop the pump as well as the load, so the trapped pressure ends up well above relief — and the tube is checked for it."

Step 10 — Ports and lines

Asks: the smallest drilled diameter in each port.

Example: 20 mm ports. The cap port carries the big 92.4 L/min on the return stroke: 4.90 m/s — inside both published guidelines (up to 5.5 and 6.1 m/s), so OK. The rod port: 2.50 m/s.

Say it out loud: "We size the cap port for the return flow, which is almost twice the pump flow."

Step 11 — Dynamics

Asks: oil stiffness (bulk modulus), dead oil volume in lines, and optionally how fast you want to accelerate.

Plain meaning: oil is a stiff spring, not a solid. Load on that spring has a natural "bounce" frequency; low means jerky, bouncy motion.

Example: lowest bounce is 40.7 Hz, at 512 mm out. No check runs unless you enter an acceleration frequency (it should be at most a quarter of this).

Say it out loud: "The oil acts like a spring; this tells us how stiff and steady the cylinder will feel when it moves the load."

Step 12 — Head and piston retention

This step answers two questions a released drawing usually rates: can the gland be pushed out of the tube, and can the piston strip off the rod?

  • Loads. The piston joint carries relief pressure × the rod-side area A2 on retraction. The gland carries the highest rod-side pressure — relief, or the intensified pressure if the rod side can be metered out — × A2.
  • Threads. Enter the engagement length and four limit diameters from the thread class (SPC Tools lists ISO 965 and ASME B1.1 limits): internal minor max (Kn max), external pitch min (Es min), external major min (Ds min), internal pitch max (En max). Using the limits, not the nominal size, gives the worst-case (smallest) shear area. The site works out which side strips first: the male thread shears at the female minor diameter, the female thread at the male major diameter.
  • Retaining ring. For a flat or spiral ring, enter the bore at the groove, ring thickness, groove depth and the ring maker's shear strength. The lower of ring shear and groove first-yield governs. Round-wire rings are not covered yet.
  • Bolted head. Use step 8 with construction set to bolted.

Strength here is shear yield (0.577 × yield), and the shear areas are geometric minimums; real threads carry somewhat less, so hold your normal safety factor.

The FEA tab

A small finite-element model of the whole cylinder, built from the numbers you already typed in the Design steps — nothing to re-enter. It treats the tube and rod as a bendable beam joined at the gland and shows three pictures: how far the cylinder sags sideways, the bending moment, and the stress along its length, plus the load at which the whole thing would buckle.

Example: lying horizontal, its own weight (tube + oil 0.532 kN/m, rod 0.296 kN/m) plus the 157.08 kN stall push bends it only 0.169 mm; the push magnifies the bending 1.152 times; the highest stress is 47 MPa in the rod — 41 MPa of that is the plain push. The whole cylinder would buckle at 1,188.25 kN, 7.56 times the stall push.

Why trust it: the "How it is verified" table on the tab shows it reproducing textbook answers exactly and a published research result to within 0.02 %. Add a side load or the gland stiffness Kf to see how much worse real-world misalignment and play make it.

Say it out loud: "This is a finite-element model of the cylinder itself — it uses our design numbers, matches the textbook cases, and shows the sag and stress along the whole length."

Check it against a drawing you trust

Before trusting the site on a new design, run a cylinder you already know through it and compare. A released, signed-off drawing with ratings on it is the best test.

  1. Switch units to match the drawing (in · psi or mm · bar).
  2. Enter bore, rod, stroke, retracted pin-to-pin length, tube OD and the pressures. Pick the mounting and end fixity that match the drawing (two clevises or eyes = pinned – pinned, K = 1.0).
  3. Enter the real material yields and modulus from the bill of materials — not the example values. Steel modulus is about 30 Msi (206 GPa); typing 30000 into an Msi field is the classic slip, and the report now flags it.
  4. Set the cushion lengths to 0 if you do not know them, so the example cushions do not change the tube check.
  5. Compare.

What it does not do (honest limits)

  • The one caution: for buckling, the rod-only Euler / Johnson check governs. DNV's whole-cylinder formula reads about 0–18 % high compared with the exact answer — DNV's own factor of 4 covers that, so read DNV only against its 4. The rigid-joint model is high too, because real glands have play.
  • Not checked yet: how the head is held in (thread, wire ring, bolts); thread stripping; the rod eye; seal friction; fatigue life; peak (not average) cushion pressure; ISO/TS 13725 and NFPA T3.6.37 buckling methods; DNV mounting rules other than pin–pin.
  • It checks the cylinder, not the valves, hoses, pump or machine.
  • Material strengths are yours to enter; the loaded ones are examples.
  • It is a design check, not a certificate — final designs still go against the customer spec, the cylinder standard and the suppliers' data.

How a hydraulic cylinder works

A cylinder turns fluid pressure into a straight-line force. Oil pumped into one side of the piston pushes it; oil on the other side flows out to tank. The force is simply pressure times the area the pressure acts on.

F = p × A    A = π·D²/4    speed v = Q / A
  • Extending, pressure acts on the full bore area A1 — the strongest, slowest direction.
  • Retracting, pressure acts only on the annulus A2 = A1 − rod area — weaker but faster for the same flow.
  • The area ratio φ = A1/A2 sets how different the two directions are. A large rod gives a high φ: more buckling strength, less pull force, faster retract.
  • Oil coming out of the cap side on retract is φ times the pump flow — size the cap port and return line for it.
  • Intensification: if the rod side is blocked or metered while the cap side is pressurised, the rod side can reach p × φ — above the relief setting. Seals, hoses and the head must take it.
  • Regeneration: connecting the rod-side outflow back to the cap side makes the cylinder extend faster (as if only the rod area were working) but with force only p × rod area.

Single- and double-acting

A single-acting cylinder is powered one way and returned by the load or a spring. A double-acting cylinder is powered both ways. A double-rod (through-rod) cylinder has equal areas both sides — equal speed and force each way, common in servo systems.

Parts of a cylinder

  • Tube (barrel) — usually cold-drawn or skived-and-roller-burnished seamless steel tube, honed for the seals.
  • Cap (bottom, blind end) — closes the tube; carries a port and often the rear mounting.
  • Head (gland) — where the rod leaves: holds the rod seal, the rod bearing (guide), the wiper and often a buffer seal.
  • Piston — carries the piston seal and wear bands; fixed to the rod by a nut, a thread or a weld.
  • Rod — usually induction-hardened and hard-chrome-plated steel, polished; carries the load out.
  • Seals — piston seal (holds pressure across the piston), rod seal, buffer seal (shields the rod seal from pressure peaks), wiper (keeps dirt out), static seals (O-rings with back-up rings).
  • Wear bands / guide rings — keep metal off metal and take side load.
  • Cushions — a spear or collar that enters a pocket near the end of stroke, trapping oil to slow the piston; often with an adjustable needle and a check valve for a quick start.
  • Ports — threaded (SAE / ISO 6149, BSPP / ISO 1179) or flanged (SAE J518 / ISO 6162).
  • Mountings and rod end — flange, clevis, trunnion, foot or tie-rod extension; threaded rod end, rod eye or spherical bearing.

The design process

  1. Define the job — forces both ways, mass, orientation, stroke, speeds, cycle rate, pressure available, environment, duty and life.
  2. Choose the pressure — working pressure from the power unit and the relief setting; higher pressure means a smaller cylinder but more stress everywhere.
  3. Size the bore — from the push force (and the pull force with the rod), with margin for friction and pressure drop; round up to a standard bore (ISO 3320).
  4. Size the rod — big enough not to buckle when pushing, fit the area ratio you want, round to a standard rod.
  5. Check speed and flow — flow each way, pump size, oil volume, power, port and line velocities.
  6. Choose the mounting — how the force goes into the machine; it sets the buckling length and the side loads.
  7. Check long strokes — buckling, the stop tube, rod sag when horizontal.
  8. Size the tube wall — against the maximum pressure, with your safety factor; check bore growth against seal extrusion gaps.
  9. Design the ends — cap thickness, welds or tie rods, head retention, port bosses.
  10. Design the rod end and pins — thread, eye, pin shear and bearing.
  11. Cushioning — if speeds or masses are high, stop the load at the end of stroke without shock.
  12. Select seals and materials — pressure, speed, temperature, fluid, friction, life.
  13. Tolerances and finishes — bore and rod fits and surface finish from the seal maker; chrome thickness and hardness.
  14. Dynamics (servo use) — natural frequency, stiffness, friction.
  15. Test and document — proof and leak tests, cushion and function tests, drawings and inspection plan.

The Design tab follows this order.

Seals

Seals decide leakage, friction and life. Each has a job:

  • Rod seal — keeps oil in as the rod moves out; must also let the oil film back in on the return stroke.
  • Buffer seal — sits in front of the rod seal and takes the pressure peaks, so the rod seal lasts.
  • Wiper — scrapes dirt and water off the rod; double-lip wipers also catch the oil film.
  • Piston seal — holds pressure across the piston; single-acting or double-acting. Drift under load usually starts here.
  • Wear bands — carry side load; never let the seal do the guiding.

Extrusion gap

Under pressure, a seal tries to squeeze into the clearance behind it. The gap grows with pressure (the tube balloons — step 5 shows the bore growth) and with wear and side load. Seal makers give a maximum gap for each pressure; back-up rings and harder materials resist extrusion.

Use the seal maker's housing tables (groove sizes, fits and surface finish) — ISO 5597 and ISO 7425 standardise many housings.

Mountings

The mounting decides how the force reaches the machine, how long the column is for buckling, and whether side loads are taken by the cylinder or the machine.

Fixed, on the centrelineHead or cap flange, tie-rod extensionsBest for straight-line thrust; the machine must align the rod

  • Pair a pivoting mount with a pivoting rod end, in the same plane — otherwise the rod is bent.
  • Side loads wear the bearing and seals; guide the load separately.
  • Long horizontal strokes: the rod and tube sag under their own weight; supports or a stop tube help.

Materials and finishes

  • Tube: seamless cold-drawn or skived and roller-burnished steel tube, finished to a honed bore; stainless or aluminium for special cases. Weldability matters for welded bodies.
  • Rod: medium-carbon or alloy steel, induction-hardened under hard chrome for dent resistance; stainless, nitrided, nickel-chrome or laser-clad rods for corrosion. Chrome thickness, hardness and crack density are specified on the drawing.
  • Heads, caps, pistons: steel or ductile iron; bronze or polymer bearings in the head.
  • Pins and bushes: hardened alloy steel pins, greaseable bushes or spherical bearings.

Look up minimum yield strengths for your grades in Materials Atlas and enter them in the design steps — the app never assumes a material.

Tolerances and surface finish

Bore and rod diameters are held to ISO fits chosen by the seal maker for the seal type and pressure; surface finish is specified as Ra together with Rz / Rmax and the material ratio Rmr — too rough wears seals, too smooth starves them of oil film. Take the numbers from your seal supplier's housing tables.

Use SPC Tools for ISO 286 fit limits, thread gauges, surface finish conversions and GD&T.

  • Rod straightness and chrome thickness are checked before assembly.
  • Concentricity of head, piston and tube controls seal life.
  • Thread fits on the rod end and ports follow the thread standard (ISO 965, ASME B1.1).

Testing and inspection

  • Proof (static) test — hold a pressure above the rating and look for leaks and permanent deformation; the test pressure comes from your standard or customer.
  • Leak tests — external leakage at seals and joints; internal (bypass) leakage across the piston.
  • Function test — full stroke both ways, minimum operating pressure (breakaway), cushion adjustment, stroke length.
  • Fatigue rating — cyclic pressure testing of a design to prove its rated fatigue pressure.
  • Incoming and in-process inspection — tube bore size, roundness and finish; rod diameter, straightness, chrome thickness and hardness; thread gauging; weld inspection (visual, MT / PT, UT); cleanliness of the assembled cylinder.

Acceptance tests for hydraulic cylinders are standardised in ISO 10100; fatigue-pressure testing of fluid-power components in ISO 10771-1.
